# Script Craft

Load in Phase 5. A great-looking ad with a weak script fails; a plain-looking ad with a
strong script often works. The script is where the persuasion actually lives.

You arrive here with a concept locked in Phase 2c: one claim, one register, one proof shot,
and a known ending. This file turns that into a beat sheet. If any of the four is still open,
go back — writing is not where you decide what the ad is.

---

## The numbers that govern short-form ads

These are the constraints every decision below answers to.

**Hook rate** — share of viewers still watching at 3 seconds. Median around 28% on Meta and
33% on TikTok; top performers reach 45–55%. **Below 15% on Meta, the creative is dead** —
don't fix the middle, replace the opening.

**Length by platform.** Click-through peaks at 15–30s on Meta and 9–15s on TikTok. Past 60s
performance collapses everywhere. Note that completion rate peaks *shorter* than
click-through — under 15s completes best (around 42% on TikTok, 32% on Meta). If the goal is
reach, go shorter; if it's clicks, 15–30s on Meta is the sweet spot.

**Sound.** 80–85% of Meta viewing is muted. TikTok is structurally sound-on. **Burned-in
captions are mandatory for Meta and advisable everywhere.** Never let a joke, a claim or a
CTA exist only in audio.

The one exception is the **sensory / ASMR** register, where sound *is* the product. It runs
on sound-on placements only, carries an on-screen "sound on" cue in the first half-second,
and needs a separate cut for Meta that survives muted. Details in `creative-registers.md`.

## Structures that work in under 60 seconds

Pick one. Don't blend.

### Problem → Agitate → Solve (PAS)
The workhorse. Show the annoyance, make it briefly worse, resolve with the product.
Best for: functional products with a clear pain. Length 15–30s.

```
0–2s   the problem, visually, mid-annoyance
2–5s   it's worse than you thought
5–10s  product enters and resolves it
10–14s the result, enjoyed
14–15s CTA
```

### Hook → Demo → Proof → CTA
Direct response. Grab, show it working, evidence, ask.
Best for: gadgets, apps, tools, anything demonstrable. Length 15–30s.

### Before / After
The strongest structure in beauty, fitness, cleaning and home improvement — the transformation
*is* the argument. Best executed as one continuous move rather than a cut, so it reads as
honest. Length 9–20s.

### Single Continuous Moment
One unbroken action: the pour, the unboxing, the bite, the door opening. No cuts, no
narration. Works because it's inherently credible.
Best for: food, gloss products, luxury. Length 6–15s.
Run it in real time with the sound isolated and no music and it becomes the **sensory /
ASMR** register — same structure, sound doing the persuading.

### Comic Premise
Setup, escalation, turn, product as resolution. See `creative-registers.md`. Length 15–20s.

### Mood Piece
Atmosphere, product as object of desire, brand mark. No structure in the argumentative sense.
Best for: fragrance, jewellery, property, fashion. Length 15–30s.

### Listicle / Rapid Cuts
"Three reasons…" — fast, caption-driven, works muted. Cheap to produce and easy to vary
for A/B. Best for: apps, services, multi-feature products. Length 12–20s.
Cut to a track with one repeating graphic device and it carries the **hyper-stylised
graphic** register.

### Making Sequence
Raw → worked → changed → finished. The transformation is the argument and no claim is stated.
The viewer must feel time passing, so each stage needs a visible change, not a new angle.
Best for: food with a kitchen behind it, handmade goods, anything where price is the
objection. Register: process / craft documentary. Length 15–30s.

### Format Borrow
The ad adopts another format's grammar wholesale — an interview, a news report, a 1994
infomercial, a safety briefing — and plays it straight. The recognition *is* the hook, so it
must be legible in the first second, and the product has to belong inside the format rather
than interrupt it.
Best for: challenger brands, categories whose own advertising is uniform. Registers:
mockumentary, retro pastiche. Length 15–30s.

### Wrong World
One impossible rule, established in frame one, never explained, everyone behaving normally.
No setup and no punchline — the strangeness is the state, not an event. The product is the
only sane object on screen, and the last two seconds are plain and legible.
Best for: saturated categories, undifferentiated products, brand campaigns on organic feeds.
Register: absurdist / surreal. Length 15–20s.

## The hook: first 1–3 seconds

More important than everything after it combined. If the hook fails, the rest never happens.

**Rules.**
- **Never open on a logo.** Nobody scrolling cares. Earn the logo.
- Open on the most visually surprising frame you have. Start *in* the action, not before it.
- Assume muted. The hook must work as silent images.
- No slow build, no fade-in, no establishing shot. Frame one is already the ad.
- If a face speaks, they're already mid-sentence.

**Hook types, and what they're good for.**

| Type | Mechanism | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Visual disruption** | An arresting or unexpected image | Food, gloss products, anything beautiful |
| **Problem recognition** | The viewer's own annoyance, on screen | Functional products, cleaning, tools |
| **Pattern interrupt** | Breaks the platform's visual convention | Crowded categories, challenger brands |
| **Direct question** | Names the viewer's situation | Services, courses, B2B |
| **Bold claim** | A specific, surprising number or promise | Tech, supplements, performance products |
| **Mid-action open** | Starts as if you missed the beginning | UGC, testimonial, demo |
| **Contrast cut** | Before and after within one second | Beauty, fitness, cleaning, renovation |
| **Curiosity gap** | Shows a result, withholds the method | Apps, hacks, techniques |

**Writing a hook — three passes.** Write ten. Discard the eight that describe the product.
The two that remain will be about the *viewer*. Then check each muted: does it still work as
a silent image?

**Hook failures.** Opening on branding. Opening on a wide establishing shot. Any fade.
Explaining before showing. A hook whose punch is audio-only. A hook so unrelated to the
product that the transition feels like a bait-and-switch.

## Voiceover copy

**Write for the ear, not the eye.** Short sentences. Contractions. One idea per breath.
Read it aloud — if you stumble, rewrite it.

- Roughly 2.5 words per second is a natural pace. 15 seconds is about 35–40 words. **Most
  scripts are overwritten by half.**
- Silence is a tool. Let the proof shot play without narration.
- Never describe what's on screen. If the viewer can see it, the VO should be saying
  something else.
- Second person. "You" not "our customers."
- Specific beats vague: "twenty-two minutes" not "impressively fast."
- No announcer voice. Plain, warm, unhurried.

**Delivery notes to include in the prompt when generating VO or avatar speech:** pace,
warmth, where to pause, which word takes the emphasis. Models flatten emphasis unless told.

## On-screen text

Text does the work audio can't, because most viewers are muted.

- **One idea per card.** Never a sentence that needs re-reading.
- Large enough to read on a phone at arm's length — bigger than feels necessary on a desktop.
- **Inside the safe zone.** TikTok and Instagram cover different parts of the frame; exact
  margins in `platforms.md`. This must be designed in at the composition stage — it cannot
  be fixed later.
- High contrast against whatever is behind it. If the background is busy, add a scrim or
  reserve a deliberately dark zone in the shot when generating.
- Timed to appear *just after* the image it comments on, not simultaneously — the eye needs
  a beat to read the picture first.
- Captions for speech: word-by-word or short phrase groups, styled like native platform
  captions rather than brand typography (unless the register is straight commercial).

## The CTA

One CTA, late, specific.

- **Place it in the last 2–4 seconds.** Earlier and it interrupts persuasion; later and it's
  cut off.
- **"Link in bio" is not a CTA** — it's a location. Give a reason to move now: what they get,
  and why today.
- Verb first: "Try it free for a week." "Book Saturday's slot." "Get 20% before Friday."
- One action only. Two CTAs mean zero.
- Say it *and* show it, because of muted viewing.
- Match the funnel: cold traffic gets a low-commitment ask (watch, learn, try); warm traffic
  gets the purchase ask.
- Arthouse register: the brand mark alone is the CTA. Asking directly breaks the spell.
- High-variance registers — absurdist, sensory, retro, graphic — earn attention but lose
  recall. Their last two seconds must be flat and legible: product, name, one line. Test it
  by showing only those two seconds to someone; if they can't name the product, the ad
  bought attention and spent it on nothing.

## Producing variants

For several accounts you need real variants, not re-crops. What to change, in order of impact:

1. **The hook** — highest-leverage single change. Same body, three different openings.
2. **The register** — UGC version and polished version of the same claim. **The Phase 2b
   slate is already this list**: the concepts the user didn't pick are the strongest variant
   set you will ever have, because they were built to differ. Before inventing variants,
   look at what you already showed them.
3. **The claim** — lead with a different benefit.
4. **Length** — a 9s cutdown of a 20s ad performs differently, not just shorter.
5. **The CTA** — different offer or urgency.

Change one variable at a time if you want to learn anything. Log each variant in the registry
(`campaign.py`) so the same cut never lands on two accounts — overlapping audiences read
duplicates as spam.
