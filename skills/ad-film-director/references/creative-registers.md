# Creative Registers

Load in Phase 2b. You need this file for two jobs: to build the concept slate, and to choose
the register once a concept is picked. Choosing the register is a strategic decision, not a
matter of taste — a technically flawless ad in the wrong register fails.

Ten registers. Four of them cover most commercial short-form work; the other six exist
because "the obvious ad, done well" is not the only thing a product can have, and a slate
built from four buckets is not a slate.

**If you are here to build a slate, the method is at the bottom of this file** — *Building
the slate*. Read the dial below first, then skim the registers, then go there.

---

## The conventionality dial

Conventionality is a dial, not a mood — one number, 0–10, declared per concept. Same shape as
the intensity dials in the `design-craft` skill, and named to match: high means far from the
default, low means close to it.

| Dial | Range | 0 means | 10 means |
|---|---|---|---|
| `VARIANCE` | 0–10 | The ad this category already makes, made well | The ad nobody in this category has made |

It is not a quality scale. A 2 can be excellent and a 9 can be worthless. It exists so a
slate can be spread on purpose rather than by vibe: you pick points on the dial *first*,
then find the concept that lives there.

Each register has a native band. You can push a register a point or two outside its band —
an absurdist premise shot as straight commercial sits lower than absurdism usually does —
but if you find yourself pushing four points, you have chosen the wrong register.

| Register | Band |
|---|---|
| Straight commercial | 0–2 |
| UGC | 2–4 |
| Process / craft documentary | 3–5 |
| Humour | 4–6 |
| Mockumentary | 5–7 |
| Retro pastiche | 5–7 |
| Sensory / ASMR | 5–7 |
| Arthouse | 6–8 |
| Hyper-stylised graphic | 7–9 |
| Absurdist / surreal | 8–10 |

**Default centre of gravity: 4.** Most products want a slate centred around 4 with one
concept well below and one well above. Raise the centre for a challenger brand, a crowded
category, an organic feed, or a client who says the last agency was boring. Lower it for
regulated categories, cold performance traffic, enterprise buyers, and anything where a
mistake in tone costs a licence.

## Quick decision matrix

| If this is true | Register |
|---|---|
| Impulse purchase, low price, young audience, TikTok-first | **UGC** or **Humour** |
| Considered purchase, mid-to-high price, trust matters | **Straight commercial** |
| Luxury, fragrance, jewellery, premium property, brand-building | **Arthouse** |
| Challenger brand fighting an established leader | **Humour** or **Mockumentary** |
| Category is boring and everyone is polished | **Humour** or **UGC** |
| Regulated category (pharma, finance, health claims) | **Straight commercial** |
| Software, apps, productivity | **Humour** or **UGC** |
| Cold traffic, direct response, performance campaign | **UGC** |
| Retargeting, brand recall, high-ticket close | **Straight commercial** or **Arthouse** |
| The price is the objection and the making explains it | **Process / craft documentary** |
| Handmade, small-batch, single-origin, "why it costs that" | **Process / craft documentary** |
| The category's own advertising is the thing worth mocking | **Mockumentary** or **Retro pastiche** |
| Brand has an era it can credibly borrow, or the product is nostalgic | **Retro pastiche** |
| The product's appeal is texture, sound or ritual | **Sensory / ASMR** |
| Sound-on placement, long dwell time, TikTok or Shorts | **Sensory / ASMR** |
| No physical object at all — fintech, SaaS, insurance, a number | **Hyper-stylised graphic** |
| Everyone in the feed looks identical and nothing is landing | **Absurdist / surreal** |
| Brand is established enough that strangeness reads as confidence | **Absurdist / surreal** |

**Performance reality:** UGC-style creative outperforms polished production on hook rate
(roughly +31%) and click-through (roughly +33%) because it reads as native content rather
than as an ad. Polished production wins on brand recall and on higher-ticket purchase intent,
because production value itself signals substance. Strong advertisers run both, weighted
around 60% UGC for prospecting and 40% polished for retargeting and brand.

The high-variance registers do not have comparable public benchmark data, and you should not
invent any. What is reliably true: they win or lose harder than the middle. Route them to
organic feeds and retargeting first, where a miss costs attention rather than budget.

Never let this table override an explicit brand guideline. If the user has a defined tone,
that wins.

## 1. UGC — user-generated, or convincingly like it

`VARIANCE 2–4`

**What it is.** Shot as if by a customer on a phone. Imperfect framing, real environments,
direct address to camera, natural speech. The persuasion comes from *apparent authenticity*.

**When it wins.** Cold traffic. Impulse categories. Cosmetics and skincare — by a wide
margin the strongest performer there. Apps. Supplements. Anything a real person can
demonstrate.

**Craft rules.**
- Imperfection is the point. Slightly off-centre framing, a hand adjusting the phone, a
  natural stumble in speech. Perfect framing destroys the effect.
- Handheld, natural light, real rooms. A kitchen at 8am beats a studio.
- Open mid-thought, as if the camera started late: "…okay so I've been using this for
  three weeks and —"
- The product enters the frame the way a real person would hold it — not presented.
- Speak to one person, not an audience.
- Captions styled like native platform captions, not like brand typography.

**In AI generation.** Ask explicitly for phone-camera characteristics: slight handheld drift,
natural window light, a real domestic interior, a slightly imperfect frame. Models default to
polished cinematic output, so you must actively suppress it. Avatar tools (HeyGen and similar)
are strong here for a presenter — that's their home ground.

**Risks.** Regulated categories: an authentic-sounding personal claim is still a claim.
Never fabricate testimonials or results.

## 2. Humour

`VARIANCE 4–6`

**What it is.** A comic premise carries the product message. Persuasion through the goodwill
of having been entertained.

**When it wins.** Challenger brands. Boring categories where everyone else is earnest. Low
price points. Software. Food. Gyms. Younger audiences. Anywhere a polished ad would be
scrolled past on sight.

**Craft rules.**
- **One joke, one setup, one turn.** Fifteen seconds holds a single comic idea.
- The product should be *inside* the joke, not appended after it. If you could remove the
  product and the joke still works, the ad fails.
- Land the turn by second 6–8, then let the product be the resolution.
- Understatement outperforms mugging. Deadpan reads as confident; frantic reads as desperate.
- Exaggerate the *problem*, never the product's performance.
- Test it muted. If the joke needs audio, half the Meta audience misses it.

**Comic structures that work in 15 seconds.**
- *Escalation* — the problem gets absurdly worse, then the product ends it.
- *Deadpan mismatch* — an epic treatment of a trivial problem.
- *Expectation break* — sets up a cliché, then swerves.
- *Understated hero* — everyone panics, one person calmly uses the product.
- *Anti-ad* — the ad admits it is an ad and refuses to perform. Works only for a brand with
  enough standing that refusal reads as confidence rather than as having no budget.

**In AI generation.** Comic timing lives in the edit, not the generation. Generate slightly
longer clips than you need so you can find the beat. Facial comedy is still unreliable —
prefer situational and physical humour over subtle expression work.

**Risks.** Humour that mocks the customer. Jokes that overshadow recall — funny but nobody
remembers the brand. Cultural humour that doesn't travel.

## 3. Straight commercial

`VARIANCE 0–2`

**What it is.** Confident, well-made, unironic persuasion. Clear promise, clean
demonstration, credible tone. The default register of professional advertising.

**When it wins.** Considered purchases. Mid-to-premium pricing. Trust-dependent categories:
clinics, finance, supplements, cars, appliances. Retargeting. Enterprise software. Any
category where looking substantial is part of the pitch.

**Craft rules.**
- Lead with the benefit, demonstrate it, prove it, ask for the action.
- Production value carries meaning here: clean light, stable camera, intentional grade.
- Show the product working, not the product sitting.
- Specificity builds credibility. "Charges in 22 minutes" beats "charges fast."
- Voiceover, if used, should be plain and unhurried. Announcer-voice reads as dated.
- One claim, proven visually. A claim you show beats three you state.

**In AI generation.** This register benefits most from the highest-quality generation you
have available — it's worth routing hero shots to a manual UI on a premium platform. Physical
realism and clean product fidelity matter more here than anywhere else.

**Risks.** Blandness. The failure mode is competent and forgettable. A distinctive visual
idea is what saves it.

## 4. Arthouse

`VARIANCE 6–8`

**What it is.** Mood, atmosphere and craft carry the message. Sparse or no dialogue. The
product appears as an object of desire rather than a solution to a problem.

**When it wins.** Fragrance. Jewellery and watches. Premium spirits. High-end property.
Fashion. Brand films. Anything where the purchase is emotional and the price is high enough
that justification would cheapen it.

**Craft rules.**
- Withhold. Show less than you want to. Suggestion outperforms explanation.
- Let shots breathe: 3–4s each, sometimes longer.
- Sound design over music, or music that's genuinely good rather than stock-uplifting.
- Extreme control of light and colour. A single, committed palette.
- Text minimal. Often just the product name at the end.
- No feature list, no CTA beyond the brand mark. Asking too directly breaks the spell.

**In AI generation.** The most forgiving register technically, because abstraction and
shallow focus hide model artefacts. Slow moves, macro detail and controlled darkness all
generate well. Frequently the best register when generation quality is limited.

**Risks.** Beautiful and meaningless. If nobody can tell what's being sold, it isn't an ad.
Also fails badly on cold performance traffic — it needs an audience that already cares.

## 5. Process / craft documentary

`VARIANCE 3–5`

**What it is.** The making is the argument. Hands, materials, tools, heat, time. No claim is
stated because the labour states it. The closest thing advertising has to showing your work.

**When it wins.** Whenever **price is the objection**. Handmade, small-batch, single-origin,
slow-fermented, hand-finished, made-here. Food and drink with a real kitchen behind it.
Furniture, leather, knives, ceramics, coffee, whisky, tailoring. Also strong for industrial
and B2B, where the factory is genuinely impressive and nobody ever shows it.

**Craft rules.**
- **Hands, constantly.** A process ad without human hands is a factory tour.
- Sequence by transformation, not by beauty: raw → worked → changed → finished. The viewer
  must feel time passing.
- Real sound, no music, or music that starts only at the end. The tools make the track.
- Macro on the moment of change: the fold, the pour, the sear, the first cut, the stitch.
- Never narrate what is visible. If a VO exists it says the one thing the image cannot —
  usually a number: how long, how many, how old.
- End on the finished object in ordinary use, not on a hero pedestal shot.

**In AI generation.** Macro hands doing a repeatable physical action is one of the things
current models do best — short clips, tight framing, no faces, no text. Generate each stage
as a separate 3–4s clip and let the assembly carry the sense of duration. Avoid wide shots
of a workshop with several people; that is where the artefacts live.

**Risks.** Boring if nothing actually changes on screen — this register needs a real
transformation, not a montage of pretty tools. And do not imply craft that isn't there:
showing a hand-forge for a mass-produced object is a lie the comments will find.

## 6. Mockumentary

`VARIANCE 5–7`

**What it is.** The grammar of documentary — interviews, lower-third titles, an observed
camera that isn't supposed to be noticed — applied to something that is obviously staged.
The comedy is in the *format*, not in jokes.

**When it wins.** Challenger brands attacking a category that takes itself seriously. Any
product whose competitors advertise identically — the format itself becomes the target. B2B
and workplace products, where office-documentary is instantly legible. Also good for a brand
that wants to be funny without needing a joke to land.

**Craft rules.**
- Commit to the format completely. Sit-down interview framing, slightly off-centre, a
  practical light source in shot, a title card with a name and an absurd job description.
- **Play it entirely straight.** Nobody in a mockumentary knows it is funny. The moment a
  performer winks, it becomes a sketch and loses its authority.
- The camera reacts: a small reframe, a push at the wrong moment, a snap to something
  happening off to the side. Camera as an embarrassed third person.
- Use the talking head to say the quiet part — the objection a real customer has and no
  brand will name.
- Cutaway B-roll should be dull on purpose. Dull B-roll is what makes it read as real.

**In AI generation.** Interview framing is the single most reliable thing avatar tools do,
so this register routes cheaply: presenter shots to an avatar platform, B-roll to whatever
is automatic. Keep each interview clip under 6s. Do not attempt two named characters in one
frame — see `consistency.md`; no current model holds that.

**Risks.** It fails as pure format if there is no idea underneath — a fake interview about
nothing is still nothing. And it needs the viewer to recognise the format being parodied;
if the audience has never seen the category's advertising, the joke has no floor.

## 7. Retro pastiche

`VARIANCE 5–7`

**What it is.** The ad borrows a whole era's grammar — 1990s infomercial, 1980s VHS,
1960s Technicolor optimism, early-2000s webcam. The period is a costume worn without irony
or worn as the whole joke; either works, but pick one.

**When it wins.** A product with a genuine heritage claim. A rerelease or an anniversary. A
category the audience associates with a specific decade (fitness equipment, kitchen gadgets,
soda, video games). And as the pattern-interrupt slot on a slate: an era-correct frame in a
contemporary feed stops the thumb because it looks like a mistake.

**Craft rules.**
- **Pick one year, not a vague past.** "1987" produces decisions; "retro" produces mush.
- Get the artefacts right: aspect ratio, grain or tape noise, the era's lens character,
  interlacing, chroma bleed, the typeface family and how titles animated *then*.
- The performance must match the era too. Period image with contemporary delivery reads as
  a filter, not a pastiche.
- Modern audio under a period image is the giveaway. Either commit or use the contrast
  deliberately as the turn.
- One anachronism, used as the punchline, is worth more than a hundred period details.

**In AI generation.** Era look is something models render willingly — name the format
("shot on VHS, 4:3, tape tracking noise, soft chroma bleed") rather than the mood ("retro
vibe"). Period text and logos will come out wrong; generate clean plates and add all
typography in the edit, where you can use a real period typeface.

**Risks.** Nostalgia for an era your buyer never lived in reads as random. Rights: an era's
music, its famous ads and its trade dress belong to somebody. Evoke a period, never
reproduce a specific ad.

## 8. Sensory / ASMR

`VARIANCE 5–7`

**What it is.** Sound and texture carry everything. Extreme close-ups, recorded-feeling
audio, the ritual of using the product performed in real time. No narrative, no claim, no
VO. The viewer stays because it feels good, not because they were persuaded.

**When it wins.** Food and drink. Cosmetics and anything applied to skin. Cleaning, where
the satisfying result is the whole product. Packaging that is nice to open. Coffee, candles,
stationery, tools with good mechanisms. Strongest on TikTok and Shorts, which are sound-on
and reward dwell time.

**Craft rules.**
- **This is the one register that inverts the muted-viewing rule.** Sound is the product.
  So label it: an on-screen "sound on" cue in the first half-second, and a version that
  still works muted for Meta placement. Never ship the sound-on cut as the Meta cut.
- Real time, no cuts, or very few. Speed kills the effect.
- One action, completed. The pour finishes. The lid closes. The cream disappears into skin.
- Macro, static or barely moving camera. The subject moves, the frame does not.
- Silence around the sound: no music bed, no room tone from another room. Isolation is what
  makes a sound feel close.
- Hands only. A face turns it into a demo and breaks the intimacy.

**In AI generation.** Video models generate the image well here — macro, slow, no faces —
and generate the *audio* badly. Plan on sourcing or recording the hero sound separately and
laying it under; the crunch, the click and the pour are worth more than the picture. State
in the prompt that there is no music.

**Risks.** Dwell time without recall: people watch and never learn what it was. Always land
the product identity at the end, and never run this register on cold traffic where nobody has
a reason to stay. Also, the effect is fragile — one bad synthetic sound destroys it.

## 9. Hyper-stylised graphic

`VARIANCE 7–9`

**What it is.** Design-led rather than photographic. Flat colour fields, hard geometry,
kinetic typography, impossible transitions, the product treated as a graphic element rather
than an object in a room. Closer to a title sequence than to a commercial.

**When it wins.** When there is **no physical object** — fintech, insurance, SaaS, a
marketplace, a number. When the product is a system rather than a thing. Category leaders
who can afford to be abstract. And as a slate's high-variance option when strangeness is
wanted but surrealism would be too much.

**Craft rules.**
- One structural idea, repeated. A shape, a transition, a colour move — the ad is that idea
  three times, not three ideas once.
- Type is a character, not a caption. It enters, it acts, it leaves. Size contrast does the
  emphasis; motion does the timing.
- Two colours plus one accent. Graphic work dies of too many colours faster than any other
  register.
- Transitions carry meaning: a wipe that becomes the product's shape, a cut on a beat.
  Random motion-graphics energy reads as a template.
- Rhythm is everything and it must be cut to the track. Design the music first.

**In AI generation.** Models cannot render clean typography or precise geometric motion —
do not ask them to. Generate abstract plates, textures and product elements, then build the
graphic layer in the edit; hand off to `video-editor` for the assembly. This is the one
register where the generation platform matters least and the edit matters most, which makes
it the cheapest high-variance option on a slate when credits are tight.

**Risks.** Looking like a stock motion-graphics template, which is worse than looking plain.
And abstraction that never resolves into a product — decide early which frame is the one
where the viewer learns what this is.

## 10. Absurdist / surreal

`VARIANCE 8–10`

**What it is.** Dream logic, played straight. Something impossible happens and nobody in
the frame reacts. Unlike humour there is no setup and no punchline — there is a world with
one wrong rule in it, and the product lives comfortably inside that rule.

**When it wins.** Saturated categories where every competitor is making the same ad and
attention is the only scarce thing. Established brands with enough standing that strangeness
reads as confidence rather than as confusion. Organic feeds and brand campaigns. Products
that are genuinely undifferentiated — when the thing is the same as its competitor, the
world around it is the only place left to differentiate.

**Craft rules.**
- **One impossible rule, stated visually in the first second, never explained.** Two rules
  is chaos; explaining the rule is death.
- Everyone behaves normally. The straighter the performances, the stranger the effect.
- The product must be the *sane* object in the frame — the one thing behaving as expected.
  That is what makes the ad an ad rather than a short film.
- Ground it in a mundane place: an office, a bus stop, a kitchen. Surreal in a surreal
  setting is just visual noise.
- Land a hard, plain product identity at the end. Strangeness earns the attention; a legible
  last three seconds converts it.

**In AI generation.** This is the register AI video is unfairly good at, because the things
models do wrong — objects that behave oddly, scale that drifts, physics that isn't quite
right — are on-brief here rather than defects. Describe the impossible thing in flat physical
language, exactly as you would describe a real event. Do not write "surreal" or "dreamlike";
write what is in the frame.

**Risks.** The highest-variance register there is. It fails as "what did I just watch" with
no product recall, and it fails hard on cold performance traffic. Test the last three seconds
independently: if someone who saw only those would know what the product is, the strangeness
is safe. Also, strange is not the same as unpleasant — surrealism that disturbs attaches the
discomfort to the brand.

## Mixing registers

Usually a mistake within a single ad — mixed tone confuses. But it works well **across** a
campaign: arthouse for brand-building on the main account, UGC for prospecting on a second,
absurdist for the experimental one. The variant registry (`campaign.py`) keeps track of which
register went where.

Two reliable *within*-ad mixes:
- UGC opening as a hook, resolving into a polished product shot. Native-feeling entry,
  credible payoff.
- Any high-variance register for the first 80%, straight commercial for the last three
  seconds. Strangeness buys the attention; the plain ending banks it.

## Where concepts actually come from

Before the slate, this. A concept derived from a category produces a cliché, because the
category is what everyone else derived theirs from. A concept derived from *this product*
produces something only this product could have made.

Interrogate the product on five axes. Each one reliably yields concepts:

**Material and behaviour.** What does it physically do that nothing else in the frame does?
It melts, it snaps, it stains, it stacks, it fogs up, it warms in the hand, it makes a
specific noise when it closes. Physical singularity is the cheapest route to a shot nobody
has seen, and it generates well because it is concrete.

**The ritual of use.** Not the product — the thirty seconds around it. What does the buyer
do immediately before, and immediately after? Nobody uses a coffee grinder; they get up
before their family. The ritual is where the emotion is, and it is almost always
unadvertised.

**The moment of purchase.** Where were they standing, what were they thinking, what nearly
stopped them? The objection they had in that moment is usually the strongest available
claim, and naming it out loud is the single most under-used move in advertising.

**What it replaces.** Every purchase kills something — a habit, a worse product, a chore, a
compromise. The discarded thing is often more filmable than the product. Show the funeral,
not the coronation.

**Who is in the room.** Somebody else is affected. A partner, a colleague, a child, a
neighbour, a customer, nobody at all. The second person in the room is where conflict,
comedy and stakes come from; a product alone on a table has none of the three.

Run all five before writing anything. Four of them will produce nothing for this product;
the fifth is your ad.

## Category clichés to avoid

The shots every AI ad reaches for first, because they are the shots in the training data.
None of them is *wrong* — they became clichés by working — but if a concept's whole idea is
one of these, it is not a concept. Recognise yourself doing it.

| Category | The shot everybody makes |
|---|---|
| Burgers, fast food | The slow-motion drop, the bun landing, the ripple through the patty |
| Coffee | The pour into a white cup on a wooden table, steam rising, hands cradling |
| Cars | Coastal road at sunset, drone pulling back, wet tarmac, empty city at night |
| Shampoo, haircare | Hair flip in slow motion, water sheeting over a shoulder, before/after split |
| Skincare | Cream swatch swirling on a white surface, dewy face turning to camera |
| Perfume | Silk in wind, a figure walking away down a corridor, liquid gold, a whispered name |
| Any bottle | The rotating hero on a white infinity background |
| Watches, jewellery | Cufflink close-up, sleeve adjusted, light travelling across a facet |
| Tech, gadgets | Exploded-view assembly, the unbox on a concrete slab, blue rim light |
| SaaS, apps | Dashboard on a laptop in a bright office, cursor gliding, someone nodding |
| Fitness, gyms | Chalk in a shaft of light, tyre flip, the mirror stare, the timer hitting zero |
| Property | Drone rising over the roof, curtain billowing, keys placed in a palm |
| Fashion | Rack walk toward camera in the middle of a street, hard shadow, sudden turn |
| Supplements | Ingredients dropping into water, a sunrise run, the pill bottle backlit |
| Beer, spirits | The condensation pour, the clink, friends laughing at a table nobody eats at |
| Cleaning | The wipe revealing a clean half, satisfied hand-on-hip, blue liquid |
| Toys, kids | Slow-motion laughter in a garden, parent watching from a doorway |
| Pets | Head tilt, bowl filled, tail blur, the run toward camera in a field |

Two rules that follow:

**A cliché is allowed as a shot, never as the concept.** The bite exists because it is the
best food shot ever devised; keep it as your proof shot and put an idea around it.

**If the concept survives a swap, it is a cliché.** Substitute a competitor's product into
your concept. If it still works exactly as well, you have described the category, not this
product. This is the fastest test there is.

## Building the slate

Phase 2b. The claim is set; now you produce ideas. This section is the method.

### The rules

**Three to five concepts.** Fewer than three is not a choice; more than five is homework
for the user.

**One concept per register.** Never two concepts in the same register. Registers are how
you guarantee difference — reusing one wastes a slot.

**Spread on the dial deliberately.** At least one concept at `VARIANCE` 3 or below and at
least one at 8 or above — a five-point spread as the floor, and no two concepts on the same
number. Pick the dial positions before you pick the ideas; it is much harder to spread a
slate you have already written than to write one to a spread.

**One must be the solid obvious one.** The ad a good agency would make on a Tuesday. It is
there because it is often right, and because a slate without it reads as an agent avoiding
the work rather than exceeding it.

**At least one must be genuinely non-obvious** — a concept the user would not have arrived
at, and would not have asked for. If every concept on the slate is a thing the user could
have described themselves, you have transcribed, not directed.

**Every concept is a complete miniature scenario.** Not a label, not a direction, not a
mood. Six lines, fixed shape:

```
NAME — register · VARIANCE n
Logline      one sentence: what happens
Opens on     the literal first frame, muted
Proof shot   the shot that does the persuading
Ends         the last two seconds, including how the product is identified
Why this     why it fits THIS product and THIS buyer — one line, no flattery
```

Five of those must fit on one screen. If a concept needs a paragraph to be understood, it is
not a concept yet.

**No shopping lists.** Do not offer variations on a theme, and do not surround one obvious
favourite with four throwaways you don't believe in — a rigged slate is worse than no slate,
because the user thinks they chose. Every concept on the slate must be one you would be
willing to direct.

### It works the same for a hamburger and for a car

**It does not matter what the product is.** A hamburger, a car, a mortgage, a wrench, a
funeral home, a B2B logistics API. The product determines the *treatment* — the lighting,
the pace, the register bands, the length — but it never determines whether a full scenario
gets built. There is no product too small for a real idea and none too dull. If a product
looks unpromising, that is a signal about the concepts you have found so far, not about the
product. Go back to *Where concepts actually come from* and run the five axes again.

The only thing a difficult product changes is where the slate's centre of gravity sits. A
regulated or high-trust product gets a lower centre; a commodity in a crowded feed gets a
higher one. Both still get a full slate.

### The slate is free

The slate is text. It is produced **before** the Routing Gate (§3 of SKILL.md) and it spends
nothing — no credits, no API calls, no money. Two consequences, both absolute:

- **Never skip or shorten the slate to save cost.** There is no cost. A slate is not
  generation and must never be confused with it.
- **Never let the slate leak into generation.** Producing a slate is not permission to
  produce footage. RULE 1 stands unchanged: nothing is generated until the Routing Gate has
  been run and answered. You are showing ideas, not previews.

### The self-check before you show it

Run this on your own slate. It fails more often than you expect.

1. **Is it one idea in four costumes?** Read only the loglines. If two of them could be
   described by the same sentence at a slightly higher altitude, they are the same concept.
   Cut one and replace it, don't re-word it.
2. **Does the dial actually spread?** Something at 3 or below, something at 8 or above, no
   two concepts on the same number.
3. **Does the competitor swap kill any of them?** Drop a rival product into each concept. Any
   that survives unchanged is a category cliché wearing a register.
4. **Is anything on the cliché list above the entire idea?** Shot, fine. Concept, replace it.
5. **Would you direct all of them?** If one is filler, the slate is four concepts, so make it
   four honestly.
6. **Does each one still prove the claim?** A concept that is interesting and proves the
   wrong thing is the most expensive kind of wrong.

### Presenting it

Don't ask "what register do you want?" — most people don't think in these terms, and asking
outsources your job. Show the concepts, in dial order, low to high. Then state a
recommendation with one line of reasoning and stop.

Then commit fully to whatever they pick. A half-committed register reads as indecision.

### If the user already knows

The slate is a default, not a toll gate. If they arrive saying "make it funny", or "same as
last time", or name a reference ad, **skip the slate and lock immediately** — say in one
line that you're going straight to it and move to Phase 2c. Offer once, briefly, to sketch
two alternatives; if they decline, never raise it again in the session.

Also skip when the register is not really open: an explicit brand guideline, a regulated
category with one legal treatment, or a variant of an ad you already made together. Say why
you're skipping in half a sentence, so it reads as judgement rather than as a shortcut.
